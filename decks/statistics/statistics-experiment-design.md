# Flashcards: Statistics & Experiment Design

## Populations, Samples, and Variables

- What is the difference between a population and a sample? == A population is the full group of interest; a sample is the subset actually observed.
- What is a parameter versus a statistic? == A parameter describes a population, such as its true mean; a statistic is calculated from a sample to estimate it.
- What is the unit of observation? == The entity represented by one measurement, such as a user, request, or company; multiple observations from one entity may be dependent.
- What is the difference between categorical and numerical variables? == Categorical variables describe groups or labels; numerical variables describe quantities on which arithmetic can be meaningful.
- What is an ordinal variable? == A variable with ordered categories, such as poor, fair, and good, whose distances are not necessarily equal.
- What is selection bias? == Systematic differences between who is observed and the population of interest, making sample-based conclusions potentially unrepresentative.
- Does a larger sample automatically remove bias? == No; more data can reduce random uncertainty while leaving systematic sampling or measurement errors unchanged.

## Descriptive Statistics

- How is the arithmetic mean calculated? == Add all n observations and divide by n: mean = sum(x_i) / n.
- What is the median? == The middle value of sorted observations, or the mean of the two middle values for an even-sized numerical sample.
- When is the median often more informative than the mean? == For skewed data or data with extreme values, such as salaries; it describes a typical middle observation without being pulled strongly by outliers.
- What does variance measure? == The average squared distance from the mean; the usual unbiased sample estimator divides the sum of squared deviations by n - 1.
- What does standard deviation measure? == Spread around the mean, expressed in the same units as the observations; it is the square root of variance.
- What is the interquartile range (IQR)? == The 75th percentile minus the 25th percentile, describing the spread of the middle 50% of observations.
- What does the 95th percentile mean? == Approximately 95% of observations are at or below that value; sample quantile conventions can differ slightly.
- What is the difference between a percentage point change and a relative percentage change? == A rise from 10% to 12% is +2 percentage points but +20% relative to the original 10%.
- Why can an unweighted average of group rates be misleading? == It gives small and large groups equal influence; an overall event rate requires weighting each group rate by its denominator.

## Probability and Distributions

- What is conditional probability P(A | B)? == The probability of A given that B occurred; when P(B) > 0, it equals P(A and B) / P(B).
- What does it mean for events A and B to be independent? == Knowing one occurred does not change the probability of the other; equivalently, P(A and B) = P(A) P(B).
- Are mutually exclusive events generally independent? == No; if both have positive probability, the occurrence of one makes the other impossible.
- What is Bayes' theorem? == P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B), for P(B) > 0; it updates a prior probability using evidence.
- What is the base-rate fallacy? == Ignoring how common an event is before seeing evidence, such as assuming a positive screening result almost certainly means a rare condition is present.
- What does a Bernoulli distribution model? == One binary outcome, with success probability p and failure probability 1 - p.
- What does a binomial distribution model? == The number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials with the same success probability p.
- What characterizes a normal distribution? == A symmetric bell-shaped distribution determined by its mean and variance; real data need not follow it.
- What is the law of large numbers? == Under suitable assumptions, the sample average converges to the expected value as the sample grows; it does not fix biased sampling.
- What does the classical central limit theorem say? == For independent, identically distributed observations with finite nonzero variance, the standardized sample mean approaches a normal distribution as sample size grows.
- Does the central limit theorem make the original observations normally distributed? == No; it concerns the sampling distribution of a mean or sum, not the distribution of individual observations.

## Estimation and Confidence Intervals

- What is a sampling distribution? == The distribution a statistic would have across repeated samples generated by the same sampling process.
- What is the standard error (SE)? == The standard deviation of an estimator's sampling distribution, measuring uncertainty in the estimate rather than spread among observations.
- Standard deviation versus standard error? == Standard deviation describes variation among observations; standard error describes variation of an estimate across repeated samples.
- What is the estimated standard error of a mean for independent, identically distributed observations? == s / sqrt(n), where s is the sample standard deviation and n is the sample size.
- What happens to the standard error of a mean when sample size is quadrupled? == It is approximately halved, assuming the observation variance and sampling design stay the same.
- What does a frequentist 95% confidence interval mean? == Under the model assumptions, the interval-producing procedure covers the fixed true parameter in 95% of repeated samples.
- Does a frequentist 95% confidence interval assign a 95% probability to the parameter being inside this particular interval? == No; that probability statement requires a Bayesian posterior model, not the usual frequentist interpretation.
- What is a common approximate confidence interval for a mean? == Sample mean ± critical value × standard error; for independent normal observations with unknown variance, use a t critical value with n - 1 degrees of freedom.
- Confidence interval versus prediction interval? == A confidence interval describes uncertainty about a parameter such as the mean; a prediction interval describes uncertainty about a future observation and is usually wider.
- What does a very wide confidence interval tell you? == The estimate is imprecise; the data may still be compatible with both meaningful benefit and meaningful harm.

## Hypothesis Tests

- What are the null and alternative hypotheses? == The null specifies a reference claim, often zero effect; the alternative specifies the departure the test is designed to detect.
- What is a p-value? == Assuming the null hypothesis and test assumptions hold, the probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one.
- Is a p-value the probability that the null hypothesis is true? == No; it describes data extremeness under the null, not the probability of the null given the data.
- What is the significance level alpha? == A preselected bound on the test's probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis, under its assumptions.
- What is a Type I error in hypothesis testing? == Rejecting a true null hypothesis: a false positive conclusion about an effect.
- What is a Type II error in hypothesis testing? == Failing to reject a false null hypothesis for a particular true effect: a false negative conclusion.
- What is statistical power? == The probability of rejecting the null for a specified true effect and design; power = 1 - beta, where beta is the Type II error probability.
- Does a non-significant result prove there is no effect? == No; the study may be too imprecise to detect an effect, so examine the effect estimate and uncertainty interval.
- Statistical significance versus practical significance? == Statistical significance concerns evidence against a null; practical significance concerns whether the effect is large enough to matter in the real application.
- What is the difference between a one-sided and a two-sided test? == A one-sided test targets a prespecified direction; a two-sided test allows departures in either direction.
- Why should the direction of a hypothesis test be chosen before looking at outcomes? == Choosing the favorable direction afterward increases false-positive risk beyond the intended error rate.

## Effect Sizes and Sample Size

- What is an effect size? == A numerical measure of the magnitude of a difference or association, such as a mean difference, risk difference, or standardized mean difference.
- What is Cohen's d? == A difference in group means divided by a reference standard deviation, commonly a pooled within-group standard deviation.
- What is a minimum detectable effect (MDE)? == The effect size a planned design is powered to detect at specified alpha and power; it is not automatically the smallest effect worth acting on.
- What inputs are usually needed for a sample-size calculation? == Outcome variability or baseline rate, target effect size, significance level, desired power, allocation ratio, and dependence structure.
- How does required sample size typically change if the target detectable effect is halved? == It grows by roughly four times, holding variability, alpha, power, and the basic design fixed.
- Why does treating repeated measurements from one user as independent overstate precision? == Correlated measurements contain less independent information than the same number of measurements from unrelated users.
- What is an equivalence test? == A test designed to establish that an effect lies within prespecified practically negligible bounds, rather than merely failing to detect a difference.

## Choosing Tests and Resampling

- When is a paired analysis appropriate? == When observations are naturally matched, such as before-and-after measurements on the same people; analyze within-pair differences.
- When is an independent-samples analysis appropriate? == When the groups contain distinct independent units with no pairing; account separately for clustering if it exists.
- What does Welch's t-test compare? == Two population means using independent samples without assuming equal variances; very small or heavy-tailed samples require extra care.
- What does a chi-square test of independence assess? == Whether two categorical variables are associated in a contingency table, using a large-sample approximation with adequate expected counts.
- When can Fisher's exact test be useful? == For small contingency tables, especially 2 × 2 tables with sparse expected counts, when the chi-square approximation may be unreliable.
- What does a permutation test do? == Compares the observed statistic with values obtained by rearranging labels in ways justified by the null hypothesis or randomization design.
- What is bootstrap resampling? == Repeatedly sample with replacement from the observed data and recompute a statistic to approximate its sampling uncertainty.
- What is a percentile bootstrap confidence interval? == An interval formed from quantiles of bootstrap estimates, such as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for a nominal 95% interval; its coverage is not guaranteed in every setting.
- Why must a bootstrap respect dependence in the data? == Resampling individual rows can break user, cluster, or time dependence; use cluster or block resampling when the design requires it.
- Does a nonparametric test have no assumptions? == No; it avoids some distributional assumptions but still needs assumptions such as independence, exchangeability, or a suitable sampling design.

## Experimental Design and Causality

- What is the difference between an observational study and a randomized experiment? == An observational study records existing exposure; a randomized experiment assigns treatment by chance to support causal comparisons.
- Random sampling versus random assignment? == Random sampling helps represent a population; random assignment helps make treatment groups comparable for causal inference.
- What is a confounder? == A variable that influences both treatment or exposure and the outcome, creating a noncausal association if not addressed.
- Why does random assignment help with confounding? == It makes treatment assignment independent of pre-treatment characteristics in expectation, balancing both measured and unmeasured factors without guaranteeing exact balance in one sample.
- What is blocking or stratified randomization? == Randomizing within groups defined by pre-treatment characteristics to improve balance and often precision.
- What is cluster randomization? == Assigning whole groups, such as schools or organizations, to treatments; the analysis must account for correlation within groups.
- What is interference in an experiment? == One unit's treatment affects another unit's outcome, violating the usual assumption that units do not affect one another.
- What is blinding? == Keeping participants, experimenters, or assessors unaware of assignments where feasible to reduce expectation-driven differences in behavior or measurement.
- Internal validity versus external validity? == Internal validity concerns whether the study supports its causal conclusion; external validity concerns whether that conclusion generalizes to other people or settings.
- Why does correlation alone not establish causation? == Confounding, reverse causation, selection, or chance can produce an association without the proposed causal effect.
- What is regression to the mean? == Extreme observations tend to be followed by less extreme observations when partly caused by random variation, even without an intervention.
- What is Simpson's paradox? == An aggregate association can reverse or disappear within subgroups because group composition differs; interpretation requires understanding the data-generating process.

## Multiple Testing and Bayesian Reasoning

- Why does testing many hypotheses increase false-positive risk? == Each test offers another chance to reject a true null; the chance of at least one false rejection can greatly exceed the per-test alpha.
- What does Bonferroni correction do? == Tests each of m hypotheses at alpha / m to control the probability of at least one false rejection across the family; it can be conservative.
- What is the false discovery rate (FDR)? == The expected proportion of false rejections among all rejected hypotheses, treating that proportion as zero when there are no rejections.
- What does the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure aim to control? == FDR rather than the probability of any false positive, under its required dependence assumptions.
- What is p-hacking? == Trying analyses, exclusions, outcomes, or stopping points and selectively reporting those that look significant without accounting for the search.
- Why can repeatedly checking a fixed-horizon test and stopping at significance be invalid? == It changes the rejection procedure and increases false-positive risk; use a prespecified endpoint or an appropriate sequential method.
- What is preregistration? == Recording hypotheses, outcomes, analysis choices, and stopping rules before inspecting results to distinguish planned tests from exploratory findings.
- What are a Bayesian prior, likelihood, and posterior? == The prior encodes beliefs before the data; the likelihood describes how data arise for each parameter; the posterior combines both after observing data.
- What is a Bayesian credible interval? == An interval containing a specified amount of posterior probability for a parameter, conditional on the chosen model and prior.
- Does a Bayesian analysis remove the need for careful experimental design? == No; confounding, measurement errors, model misspecification, prior sensitivity, and decision rules still matter.
